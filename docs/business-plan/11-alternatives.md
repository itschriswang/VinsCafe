# The alternatives, ranked

**The recommendation lives here.** [01-the-verdict.md](01-the-verdict.md) argues
that the concept is sound and the container is wrong. These are the other
containers, ranked by how fast they get you to your first legitimate dollar and
how much capital they consume on the way.

## 1. A mobile coffee cart or van — **the recommendation**

**Time to first dollar: weeks. Capital: $15,000–$60,000. Regulatory load: low.**

This is the same business without the building, and it is dramatically easier on
every axis.

**How it works.** You register a **mobile food premises** with the council in
which the vehicle is **garaged** — which is exactly what your garage would be
doing, entirely lawfully, with no change of use. Once Whitehorse accepts the
registration, you may **trade anywhere in Victoria** by lodging a **Statement of
Trade** through **FoodTrader**, the statewide system. **[CONFIRMED]**

**Why it collapses the problem:**

| Garage cafe | Mobile |
| --- | --- |
| Planning permit, advertised, objections | **None** — the garage stays a garage |
| Change of use, building permit, occupancy permit | **None** |
| Fire separation, accessible path, exhaust hood | **None** |
| Trade waste agreement, grease arrestor | **None** |
| Commercial waste at the house | **None** |
| Neighbour objections | **None** — you trade elsewhere |
| CGT exposure on your home | **None** — a van is not part of your house |
| Council rates reclassification | **None** |

**And the food class drops too.** Serving **coffee and tea with milk (dairy, soy,
almond or otherwise) for immediate consumption is expressly Class 3**.
**[CONFIRMED]** Class 3 means **no Food Safety Program and no Food Safety
Supervisor required** — only basic records about suppliers and potentially
hazardous food.

So a coffee-and-matcha cart is a Class 3 business. The entire apparatus of
[03-food-registration.md](03-food-registration.md) falls away.

**Where you trade.** Markets, sports grounds, school and community events,
workplaces, private functions, and any private land with the owner's consent.
Whitehorse and the surrounding councils — Monash, Knox, Manningham, Maroondah —
are all reachable, and one registration covers all of them.

**What you give up.** The room. The Sunday-morning-at-a-place ritual is harder to
build from a cart that moves, and the pho toastie needs rethinking. But the
brand, the matcha, the coffee and the Sunday all survive intact.

**What it costs.** A secondhand cart or trailer with a good machine sits around
$15,000–$35,000; a fitted van more. **[VERIFY]** Against $56,700–$228,500 for the
garage, with a real payback rather than none.

## 2. Market stalls — the fastest possible start

**Time to first dollar: 2–6 weeks. Capital: $3,000–$15,000. Regulatory load: very low.**

A **temporary food premises** registration through FoodTrader, a Statement of
Trade lodged with each council whose area you trade in, a gazebo, a table and a
machine. **[CONFIRMED]**

This is the cheapest legitimate way to find out whether people will queue for
your matcha. Melbourne's east has a dense weekend market scene, and a stall is a
one-day-a-week commitment that matches exactly the trading pattern in
`config.js`.

Do this **first**, whatever else you decide. It answers the only question that
matters — *will strangers pay $5.00 for this matcha latte twice* — for a few
hundred dollars, before any of the expensive decisions.

## 3. Class 3 from home: packaged and low-risk only

**Time to first dollar: 4–8 weeks. Capital: minimal. Regulatory load: low.**

A **Class 3** home-based food business can be run from a domestic kitchen with
council approval. **[LIKELY]** Class 3 covers low-risk food — cakes that **do
not contain egg, custard or cream** — and the storage and sale of **pre-packaged**
potentially hazardous food. **[CONFIRMED]**

Against your board, that means: **the black sesame cookie and the cardamom bun,
probably. The basque cheesecake, the custard tart and the soft egg, no.**

Useful as a wholesale or online sideline. Not a cafe.

## 4. Wholesale to other cafes

**Time to first dollar: 1–3 months. Capital: low to moderate.**

Bake at home under a Class 3 registration where the product allows, or hire a
shared commercial kitchen where it does not, and sell to cafes that already have
the permits and the customers. Cardamom buns and sesame cookies are exactly the
kind of distinctive item a suburban cafe will buy in.

No customers on your property, no planning permit, no amenity issue.

## 5. A pop-up in someone else's registered premises

**Time to first dollar: 2–8 weeks. Capital: very low.**

Take over an existing registered cafe on its closed day — a great many suburban
cafes shut on Mondays, and a fair few on Sundays. The premises are already
registered, already compliant, already have the toilets and the grease trap.

This is the single best way to run **the actual concept**, unchanged, including
the full Class 2 kitchen, without owning any of the compliance. You would need
the host's food registration arrangements sorted properly with the council — ask
Whitehorse how they want it structured. **[VERIFY]**

For a Sunday-only cafe, this deserves serious thought. It is very close to the
original vision, and it costs a fraction of the garage.

## 6. A small commercial shopfront

**Time to first dollar: 3–9 months. Capital: high. Regulatory load: moderate.**

The conventional answer. In a commercial zone, a cafe is a permitted or
straightforwardly permit-able use, the building is already Class 6, and the
*Retail Leases Act 2003* gives you protections a home never will.

The problem is rent, and rent is what a one-day-a-week business cannot carry —
the ATO benchmark for rent is **9–14% of turnover** for a cafe, and on $41,000 of
Sunday-only revenue that supports roughly $4,000–$5,700 a year of rent. No
tenancy in Vermont South costs that. **A shopfront only works if you trade more
days**, which is a different business from the one on this website.

## 7. Online and click-and-collect

**Time to first dollar: weeks. Capital: very low.**

Note the useful detail from [02-planning-permit.md](02-planning-permit.md):
Clause 52.11 bars selling bought-in goods from the land, **except goods offered
for sale online**. **[CONFIRMED]** So the *Take home* section of the board —
beans, matcha, filter papers — is lawfully sellable online in a way it is not
lawfully sellable over a garage counter.

Delivery platforms are a poor fit: commissions of 20–35% against a 62% gross
margin **[VERIFY]** would consume more than half the margin, and they destroy the
scarcity mechanic that the whole concept rests on.

## What I would actually do

**Now:** market stall or two, to test demand. Title search and insurance call in
parallel, because they are cheap and either can kill the garage plan outright.

**Months 1–6:** a cart, registered as a mobile food premises at your own address.
Class 3, coffee and matcha. Build the audience the website already has a voice
for.

**Months 6–18:** if demand is real, negotiate a Sunday pop-up in an existing
registered venue and run the full board — toasties, prawn rolls, soft eggs — with
somebody else's grease trap.

**Year 2–3:** only then, with demonstrated trade and real numbers, decide whether
the garage conversion or a small shopfront is worth it. By then you will know
whether the queue exists, which is the one thing no amount of planning can tell
you.
