# Title, covenants and the mortgage

**Bottom line: a single line on your title can end this project independently of
anything the council decides, and finding out costs about $50 and a day. Do it
first.**

## The restrictive covenant

Many Melbourne residential subdivisions carry **restrictive covenants** — private
agreements registered on title, enforceable by the owners of the other lots in
the subdivision. Common forms include single-dwelling covenants, materials
covenants, and, critically here:

> *shall not carry on or permit to be carried on upon the land any trade or
> business whatsoever*

Vermont South is substantially post-war and later subdivision, exactly the era
and type where such covenants are common. **[VERIFY — this is a fact about your
specific title, not a generalisation you can rely on either way.]**

### Why this outranks the council

Under **section 61(4) of the *Planning and Environment Act 1987***, a responsible
authority **cannot grant a planning permit that would authorise anything that
would result in a breach of a registered restrictive covenant** — unless a permit
to remove or vary the covenant is granted as well. **[CONFIRMED]**

So if such a covenant exists on your title, the council is legally barred from
granting your cafe permit, no matter how sympathetic the planner is, how few
objections you get, or how good the traffic report is. It is a hard stop.

### The 2026 reform — check this

The **Planning Amendment (Better Decisions Made Faster) Act 2026** appears to
reverse s61(4), so that an authority **may** grant a permit authorising a breach
of a registered restrictive covenant, with statutory immunity for resulting
loss. **[VERIFY — and this one really matters.]**

Whether that provision has **commenced**, and in what form, materially changes
this page. As at the time of writing this could not be confirmed against the
legislation itself. If it is in force, a covenant becomes an obstacle rather
than a wall. If it is not, it remains a wall.

**This is the single most important thing on this page to check with a property
lawyer.** It is also new enough that a lawyer will actually need to look it up
rather than answer from memory.

### How to find out

1. Order a **title search** and the **plan of subdivision** through LANDATA
   (`landata.vic.gov.au`) or a conveyancer. Around **$20–$50** and typically same
   day. **[VERIFY]** current fee.
2. Read the **encumbrances** section of the Certificate of Title. Covenants
   appear there, usually as a reference to a dealing number.
3. Order the **instrument** creating the covenant and read the actual wording.
   The exact words matter enormously — "trade or business" is broad; some
   covenants only restrict "noxious or offensive trade", which a cafe may not be.

If you do nothing else from this entire plan this week, do this.

### If a covenant exists

Removing or varying one is difficult and usually expensive:

- A planning permit application to remove or vary the covenant, under the
  relevant provisions of the *Planning and Environment Act*, or an application
  under the *Subdivision Act 1988*, or a Supreme Court application. **[LIKELY]**
- The beneficiaries are typically **every other lot owner in the subdivision** —
  potentially dozens of households, each of whom can object.
- The practical odds are poor if even one beneficiary objects, and the tests are
  demanding.

Treat a "no trade or business" covenant as **fatal to the garage cafe** for
planning purposes, and go straight to [11-alternatives.md](11-alternatives.md).

Note that a covenant restricts what happens **on the land**. A mobile food
business that is merely garaged there, and trades elsewhere, is a materially
different question — worth putting to a lawyer, because it may be the difference
between "no" and "yes, just not here".

## Section 173 agreements

Separately, the council can **impose** a s173 agreement as a permit condition —
a registered agreement binding you and future owners to specified obligations
(hours, patron caps, parking management, and so on). **[LIKELY]**

This is not necessarily bad; it is often how a council makes itself comfortable
granting a marginal permit. But understand that it **runs with the land**, is
registered on title, and shows up when you sell. Removing one later requires the
council's agreement.

## Owners corporation

Unlikely for a standalone house on a court, but check the title. If there is an
owners corporation, its rules very commonly prohibit business use, and the OC is
another party with standing to object.

## The mortgage

If the property is mortgaged, the loan terms almost certainly contain
obligations about **use of the security property** and **not altering it
materially without consent**. **[VERIFY against your actual loan contract.]**

The questions to put to your lender, in writing:

- Does converting part of the dwelling to a registered commercial food premises
  breach my loan terms?
- Do you require consent for a change of use, and will you give it?
- Does a registered commercial use on the title change how you classify or value
  the security — and could it affect my rate, my LVR, or trigger a review?

A residential mortgage is priced on a residential security. A property with a
commercial food premises registered against it is not straightforwardly that.
Most lenders will be relaxed about a small home business; a garage with an
occupancy permit as a Class 6 building and a trade waste agreement is further
along the spectrum than a home office, and worth asking about explicitly rather
than assuming.

## Land tax and the SRO

Your principal place of residence is exempt from Victorian land tax. Using part
of it for a business **may** affect that exemption, at least proportionally.
**[VERIFY with the State Revenue Office.]** Worth a phone call, because it is
another recurring cost that does not appear in anyone's first budget.

## Resale

Two effects worth weighing:

- A registered commercial use, a s173 agreement, or an unpermitted change of use
  are all **disclosable** on sale and all narrow the buyer pool.
- Conversely, a **legitimately permitted** small commercial space attached to a
  house is a genuine asset to some buyers.

The one to avoid at all costs is the unpermitted version — a garage converted
into a commercial kitchen without permits is a defect that surfaces at the worst
possible moment.

## Ask a property lawyer or conveyancer

- Order the title and any covenant instrument, and tell me exactly what it says.
- Has the Planning Amendment (Better Decisions Made Faster) Act 2026 commenced, and does it change the s61(4) position on covenants?
- Would a covenant against "trade or business" bar a mobile food business that is garaged here but trades elsewhere?
- What are the implications of a s173 agreement for resale?
