# Other avenues

Everything so far has argued about **which container** the business goes in —
garage, cart, shopfront. That is only one of the levers. There are four:

1. **Change the address.** The permit problem is a property problem, not a
   business problem.
2. **Borrow a compliant kitchen** instead of building one.
3. **Sell the product rather than the service.**
4. **Shrink what the garage has to be** until it clears the bar.

Ranked at the end. The two most under-explored are the first and the third, and
neither has appeared anywhere else in this plan.

---

## 1. The address is a variable

This is the largest unexamined idea in the whole plan.

[02-planning-permit.md](02-planning-permit.md) turned up a **Section 1 — permit
not required** pathway for food and drink premises in a residential zone.
**[LIKELY]** The conditions are locational:

- the land is **within 100 metres of a Commercial Zone or Mixed Use Zone**, and
- has the **same street frontage** as that commercial land, and
- **adjoins, or has access to, a road in a Road Zone**.

Katupna Ct fails all three, which is why the garage needs a discretionary permit
and why that permit is the hardest part of this project.

**But those conditions describe a real and findable kind of property.** A house
on the corner near a suburban shopping strip — Vermont South Shopping Centre,
Hanover Road, Canterbury Road, Burwood Highway — could satisfy them. In that
case the entire planning fight disappears: no discretionary application, no
advertising, no objectors, no VCAT.

Everything else in this plan still applies — Class 2 food still needs a
commercial kitchen, the building still changes use, the fitout still costs what
it costs. But **the single most likely reason this project fails simply stops
existing.**

What to do with that:

- Take the three conditions to a town planner and ask them to identify streets in
  Whitehorse where a residential property would satisfy them. It is a map
  exercise and it is an hour of their time.
- Then ask whether any such property is for sale or lease.
- **Verify the conditions first.** They came from a search summary of the zone
  provisions, not the ordinance itself. If the Section 1 pathway does not read
  the way it is described here, this whole section collapses — so confirm it
  before getting attached. **[VERIFY]**

Renting a house that qualifies is a smaller commitment than converting the one
you own, and it does not put your own home's CGT exemption at risk. See
[06-structure-tax-gst.md](06-structure-tax-gst.md).

## 2. Borrow a kitchen that already complies

The garage costs $63,700–$258,500 to become a compliant Class 2 kitchen. You can
rent one by the hour instead.

| Option | Cost | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Shared / commissary kitchen** | **$32–$45/hr** for members, ~$115/hr casual venue hire | Fully fitted, council-compliant. **[LIKELY]** Providers around Footscray, North Melbourne, Port Melbourne, Coburg; minimum commitments from ~10 hrs/month |
| **Neighbourhood house / community centre** | Usually cheaper again | Many have full commercial kitchens, often underused midweek. Councils list them — Boroondara publishes a "hire a commercial kitchen" page **[CONFIRMED]** |
| **A registered cafe's closed day** | Negotiated | The pop-up route from [11-alternatives.md](11-alternatives.md) |

A commercial kitchen rental is described as getting a food business "fully
licensed and operational in **2–4 weeks**" without building anything.
**[LIKELY]**

**Why this matters more than it first looks:** it is the only option on the table
that runs **the full board, legally, including the eggs and the prawns**, without
anyone spending six figures. The kaya toast and the chilli prawn roll are the
menu's identity, and this is the cheapest lawful way to keep them.

Note you must be registered under the Food Act to hire most of these — the
venue's registration does not automatically cover your business. **[LIKELY]**
Ask each venue how they structure it.

Check what exists in Whitehorse and the neighbouring councils specifically;
everything found here was inner-west and inner-north, which is a long drive at
5am with a car full of dough.

## 3. Sell the product, not the service

The whole plan has assumed you sell **a made drink to a person standing in front
of you**. That is the most regulated, least scalable thing you can do with
matcha.

### Online retail is the lightest-touch food business available

Storage and sale of **pre-packaged low-risk food such as packaged tea** requires
only that you **notify** the council — a notification form, not a registration.
**[CONFIRMED]** That is Class 4, the bottom rung: no Food Safety Program, no
supervisor, no inspection regime of the kind Class 2 carries.

And it is expressly permitted from home. Clause 52.11 bars selling bought-in
goods on the land — **except goods offered for sale online**. **[CONFIRMED]**

So the *Take home* section of the board — beans, matcha, filter papers — which is
the one part that **cannot** lawfully be sold over a garage counter, is the part
you can start selling **this month**, from the house, with a notification form.

Requirements are labelling rather than construction: name, ingredients,
allergens, weight, date marking, lot identification, supplier details, and
compliance with Part 1.2 of the Food Standards Code. **[CONFIRMED]** If you
resell sealed tins in the supplier's own packaging, most of that is already done.

### Where it could go

- **A matcha subscription.** Recurring revenue, no premises, no Sunday, no
  neighbours. The single most scalable thing in this document.
- **Wholesale matcha to cafes.** You are a *distributor*, not a food producer —
  no kitchen at all. Given that few suburban cafes do matcha properly, and you
  would be sourcing and tasting seriously, this is a real niche.
- **The cart becomes marketing for the product**, rather than the product.

There is a pricing problem to fix first. Ceremonial matcha retails at $40–45 per
30g in Australia and the board sells 30g at $18 — see
[18-home-kitchen-and-the-cart.md](18-home-kitchen-and-the-cart.md). Sort out what
grade you are actually selling before building a retail line on it.

### Sell the knowledge too

Matcha **workshops and tastings** — how to whisk, what grade means, why
temperature matters — are a legitimate revenue line that trades on exactly the
expertise the site claims. Run them at a hired space or a partner venue. Corporate
wellness sessions are the same product with a bigger budget. Food handling rules
still apply if people consume what is made, so check the framing. **[VERIFY]**

## 4. Shrink the garage until it fits

If the garage stays the goal, make it a smaller ask. Each of these lowers the
height of the bar:

- **Drinks only.** Drop the kitchen section and the cream desserts and the
  premises becomes **Class 3**, not Class 2 — no Food Safety Program, no
  supervisor, and a far lighter fitout. This is the single biggest cost
  reduction available to the garage plan.
- **No seating — a serving window to the driveway.** With nobody accommodated
  inside, the patron-toilet question in [04-building-and-access.md](04-building-and-access.md)
  gets easier still, the fitout shrinks, and the amenity impact you are asking
  neighbours to accept is much smaller. Whether council treats a takeaway window
  from a dwelling differently from a sit-down cafe could not be established here
  and is worth asking directly. **[VERIFY]**
- **Cap the room at twenty** if there is seating — the F4 threshold.
- **Volunteer hours, patron numbers and a no-amplified-music condition** in the
  application rather than waiting to have them imposed.

A drinks-only, twenty-seat or window-only Sunday operation is a *dramatically*
more grantable proposal than a sit-down cafe grilling prawns. It is also much
closer to what the cart does — which raises the honest question of whether the
garage is worth the permit at all.

## 5. Attach to somebody else's permit and footfall

The recurring theme of every cheap option: **someone near you already has the
compliance and the customers.**

- **Gyms, yoga and pilates studios.** Matcha and wellness is a natural pairing
  and these venues have Sunday-morning traffic that finishes at exactly the hour
  a cafe wants it.
- **Farm gates, wineries, breweries** on the city fringe — permitted, hungry for
  weekend food, and a destination audience that already drove somewhere.
- **Sports clubs, golf clubs, church and community halls** with registered
  kitchens and a Sunday crowd.
- **Retail hosts** — a plant shop, a bookshop, a homewares store wanting a
  reason for people to linger.
- **Office buildings** — a standing weekly corporate cart booking is the most
  boring and most reliable revenue in this entire document.

These need no permit of your own, no building work, and often no rent — just a
revenue share and a good conversation.

## What this changes

The first plan concluded that the concept was sound and the container was wrong.
Having looked wider, the sharper version is:

> **Almost every route to this working involves not owning the premises.**

The garage is the only option that requires you to buy the compliance outright,
and it is the only one with no payback. Everything else — the cart, a hired
kitchen, a host venue, an online shop, a qualifying address you rent — either
borrows someone else's compliance or avoids needing any.

The cheapest experiment remains the same: **start selling something legally
within the month.** An online matcha shop is a notification form. A community
market stall is a Statement of Trade. Either one starts producing the only data
that matters — whether people want this — while every question in this plan is
still open.
