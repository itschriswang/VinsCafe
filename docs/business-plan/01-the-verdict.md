# The verdict

**Short version: the garage cafe as drawn — a public, sit-down, hot-food cafe in
a residential garage on a cul-de-sac, open one day a week — is legal in
principle but almost certainly not viable in practice. The same menu, the same
brand and the same Sunday can be made to work, but the vehicle has to change.**

This document says why, and what to do instead. Everything in it is sourced in
the briefings that follow; the confidence tags there matter, and the caveat in
[00-README.md](00-README.md) about how these facts were gathered matters more.

## The three findings that decide it

### 1. Nothing here is prohibited. It is all discretionary.

A cafe is a *food and drink premises*, and in the General Residential Zone that
is a **Section 2 use: a permit is required**, not a Section 3 use, which would
be prohibited outright. So the door is open. But "permit required" in a
residential court means a fully discretionary application, advertised to your
neighbours, decided on amenity, traffic, parking and neighbourhood character —
the four grounds on which a cafe in a cul-de-sac is weakest. See
[02-planning-permit.md](02-planning-permit.md).

There is also a second, quieter door that closes: **Clause 52.11 Home Based
Business**, the provision that lets you run a business from home without a
permit, allows the sale of *only* goods manufactured or serviced in the business
itself. Your coffee, matcha and toasties qualify. The 250g retail bags of beans
and the 30g matcha tins do not — they are bought in and resold. That part of the
board cannot ride on the home-business exemption, though it can be sold online.

### 2. Class 2 food cannot be done from a domestic kitchen.

This board has soft eggs, prawns, dairy and unpackaged ready-to-eat food. That
is a **Class 2** food business under the Food Act 1984, and the consistent
position of Victorian councils' environmental health officers is that Class 2
generally *cannot* meet the Food Standards Code in a domestic kitchen. Domestic
kitchens are for Class 3 — low-risk and pre-packaged.

So there is no version of this where the house kitchen quietly does the work.
The garage has to become a genuine commercial kitchen: commercial finishes,
dedicated hand basin with warm water, separate food prep and wash sinks,
mechanical exhaust over the grill, pest proofing, the lot. Plus a Food Safety
Program and a nominated Food Safety Supervisor. See
[03-food-registration.md](03-food-registration.md).

### 3. The economics do not survive one day a week.

This is the finding that actually kills it, and it is arithmetic rather than
opinion. Being a legal food premises costs roughly **$19,600 a year in fixed
costs before you sell anything** — insurance, registration, trade waste, grease
arrestor servicing, commercial waste, compliance statements, accounting. That
figure is essentially the same whether you open one day a week or six.

Spread over **312 trading hours a year**, it is crushing:

| | Lean | Base | Stretch |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Transactions / Sunday | 48 | 84 | 132 |
| Revenue | $23,581 | $41,267 | $64,848 |
| **EBITDA, owner unpaid** | **−$5,237** | **−$10,117** | **−$16,623** |
| Owner's effective hourly rate | −$8.39 | −$16.21 | −$26.64 |

Every scenario loses money. Worse, **the busier scenarios lose more**, because
the moment you need a second pair of hands you are paying the Restaurant Award
casual Sunday rate of $48.46 an hour plus super — about $54 all-in — and one
helper cannot generate $54 an hour of additional gross profit on a $10.23
average transaction.

Against this you would be spending **$63,700 to $258,500 of capital** to convert
the garage. There is no payback. Not a long payback — no payback.

The gross margin is not the problem. At 62.3% it is healthy, and the cost of
goods lands at 37.7% of revenue, squarely inside the ATO's published benchmark
band of 33–42% for cafes and coffee shops. The concept is sound. The *container*
is wrong.

## The two things that would change the answer

**Trade more days.** The fixed $19,600 spread over three or four days a week
starts to work, because revenue scales with days and the fixed cost does not.
But more days is exactly what a residential court will not tolerate, and what
the planning permit is least likely to grant. The building is fighting the
business model.

**Cap the room at 20 people.** A genuinely useful discovery: under NCC Part F4,
patron sanitary facilities are **not required where the building accommodates
not more than 20 persons**. Design for 20, and the accessible patron toilet —
$12,000 to $30,000, the single largest line in the fitout — disappears. If you
build anything, build it for twenty.

## What to do instead

Ranked in [11-alternatives.md](11-alternatives.md), but the headline is this:

> **A coffee cart or van, registered as a mobile food premises at your own
> address, is the same business without the building.**

Under the Victorian system you register a mobile food premises with the council
where the vehicle is *garaged* — which is exactly what your garage would legally
be doing — and then trade anywhere in Victoria by lodging a Statement of Trade
through FoodTrader. No change of use. No occupancy permit. No trade waste
agreement. No accessible toilet. No neighbour objections, because the trading
does not happen at the house.

Better still, **serving coffee and tea with milk is expressly Class 3**, not
Class 2 — no Food Safety Program and no Food Safety Supervisor required. Drop or
defer the egg-and-prawn kitchen and the entire regulatory burden collapses by an
order of magnitude.

The capital is a fraction of the garage conversion, the payback is real, and the
Sunday-morning ritual — the actual product — survives intact. Markets, sports
grounds, workplaces and events in Whitehorse and the surrounding councils are
the venue. The website already says "Sundays only"; it does not have to say
where.

## The honest recommendation

1. **Do not convert the garage yet.** Before spending anything, do the two
   cheap checks that can independently kill the plan: a **title search** for a
   restrictive covenant against carrying on a trade or business, and a call to
   your **home insurer** about business use. Either can end this for a few
   hundred dollars, which is the cheapest possible way to find out. See
   [10-title-covenants-mortgage.md](10-title-covenants-mortgage.md) and
   [08-insurance-and-liability.md](08-insurance-and-liability.md).
2. **Start as a mobile Class 3 coffee business.** Prove the demand, build the
   audience, learn the operation, earn money instead of spending it.
3. **Keep the garage as the licensed production kitchen only if and when the
   volume justifies it** — a Class 2 registration for *making* the food, with no
   public admitted to the site, avoids most of the planning and building
   problems, because the amenity impact is what the objections are actually
   about.
4. **Revisit the sit-down garage cafe in year three**, from a position of
   demonstrated trade, with real numbers to put in front of a planner.

None of this is a reason not to do it. It is a reason to do it in the order that
does not cost $200,000 to discover.
