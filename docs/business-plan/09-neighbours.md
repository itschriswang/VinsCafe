# Neighbours, noise, parking and the people who can stop this

**Bottom line: the neighbours are the real regulator. Council enforcement is
almost entirely complaint-driven, so a cul-de-sac that is happy about the cafe
is a cafe that operates, and one unhappy household on Katupna Ct can generate
years of friction over things that are individually trivial.**

## Why a court is the worst possible geometry

A cul-de-sac has no through traffic, which is exactly why people buy into one.
It also means:

- **Every car that arrives is visibly for you.** In a through street, cafe
  traffic is diluted into ordinary traffic. In a court, forty additional cars on
  a Sunday morning are unmistakably yours.
- **There is nowhere for overflow to go** except directly in front of the
  neighbours' houses.
- **Turning circles matter.** Courts have limited turning heads; cars doing
  three-point turns in front of driveways is the specific complaint councils
  hear most.
- **Emergency vehicle access** must be maintained, and parked-out courts are a
  standing issue for councils.
- **Everyone can see and hear everything.** Extraction fans, grinders, bins,
  deliveries, conversation on a driveway at 8am on a Sunday.

This is why parking is the crux of the planning application in
[02-planning-permit.md](02-planning-permit.md), and why the honest assessment is
that it is a hard application.

## Noise

Two regimes, and which applies matters. **[VERIFY]**

A commercial operation in a residential zone is generally assessed as
**commercial noise** under the *Environment Protection Act 2017* and the
Environment Protection Regulations 2021, which set permissible levels at
neighbouring residential premises by time of day. Separately, the **general
environmental duty** obliges you to minimise risk of harm from noise so far as
reasonably practicable — and that duty applies whether or not you exceed a
numeric limit. **[LIKELY]**

Sunday morning is, unhelpfully, one of the more protected periods.

### The actual sources, ranked by how much trouble they cause

1. **The extraction fan.** Continuous, tonal, runs the whole service. This is the
   number one commercial noise complaint in residential areas, and the one most
   likely to require acoustic treatment. Specify a quiet fan and an attenuator
   from the start; retrofitting is far more expensive.
2. **The grinder.** Short, sharp, repetitive, and it starts at 6am with the
   morning dial-in.
3. **Car doors and conversation.** Forty customers means eighty car door slams
   and a lot of talking, outdoors, early.
4. **Deliveries and bin collection.** A commercial waste truck in a residential
   court is conspicuous. Schedule it for the least sensitive time you can.
5. **Music.** Do not have amplified music outdoors. It adds nothing and it is
   the easiest possible complaint to make stick.

## Odour

Grilled prawns and a commercial kitchen exhaust in a suburban backyard is a
genuine amenity issue, and one that planning officers will ask about directly.
The discharge point matters — height, direction, and its relationship to
neighbouring windows and outdoor living areas. **[LIKELY]** Get this designed
rather than improvised; see [04-building-and-access.md](04-building-and-access.md).

Coffee, interestingly, is usually regarded as a pleasant smell. The grill is not.
This is another argument for the reduced-menu versions in
[11-alternatives.md](11-alternatives.md).

## What objections actually do

When the application is advertised, adjoining owners and occupiers are notified
and anyone can object. **[LIKELY]**

- Objections do not automatically defeat an application. Councils weigh them.
- But a **volume** of objections typically escalates the decision from an officer
  acting under delegation to a **council meeting**, where it becomes a political
  decision rather than a planning one.
- The grounds that carry weight are exactly the ones you are weakest on:
  **traffic, car parking, noise, amenity, and neighbourhood character**. "I don't
  want a business next door" carries little weight; "there is nowhere to park and
  the fan runs all morning" carries a lot.
- If a permit is granted over objections, **objectors can appeal to VCAT**, which
  costs months and money even when you win.

## The mitigation that actually works

In rough order of effectiveness:

**1. Talk to them first. Before you lodge anything.**
This is the highest-yield action in the entire project and it costs a morning. Go
to the eight or ten nearest households with a coffee and an explanation: what it
is, how small it is, one day a week, six hours, twenty people maximum, here is my
phone number, tell me directly if anything bothers you.

People object to being surprised far more than they object to cafes. A neighbour
who was consulted and given a direct line will ring you about the fan. A
neighbour who found out from a council notice on a pole will ring the council.

**2. Volunteer the limits before they are imposed.**
Offer the patron cap, the hours, the no-amplified-music, the no-signage. A
proposal that arrives with its own controls reads as considered, and gives the
planner something to write into conditions rather than having to invent it.

**3. Solve parking on your own land, visibly.**
Whatever off-street space exists, mark it and use it. Ask customers to walk from
the main road. Publish it on the website — [find-us.html](../../find-us.html)
already exists and is the natural place to say "please park on Hanover Road and
walk in", or whatever the local answer is.

**4. Treat the fan acoustically at design stage.**

**5. Give the neighbours something.** Free coffee, standing invitation, first
look at the cake. Cheap, and it converts the nearest households from potential
objectors into the people who tell their friends.

## What does not work

**Framing it as private, members-only, by appointment, or donation-based.**
None of these change the legal character of what you are doing. If you are
supplying food to the public in the course of a business, you are a food
business under the Food Act and a food and drink premises under the planning
scheme, whatever you call it and whether or not money changes hands in the usual
way. **[LIKELY]** Councils have seen every version of this framing.

**Starting quietly and hoping.** Enforcement is complaint-driven, which people
read as "I will probably get away with it". The problem is that when the
complaint does come, you have already spent the capital, you have no permit, and
you are negotiating from the worst possible position. An unpermitted change of
use is also a disclosure problem when you sell the house.

## Ask

Nobody official. **Ask your neighbours.** Before the council, before the planner,
before the builder. Their answer is the best available predictor of whether this
project succeeds, and it is free.
