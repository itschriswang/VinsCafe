# The home kitchen, and the cart

Two questions asked after the first plan landed. Short answers first.

**Is a "council approved home kitchen" real?** Yes, it is real and councils do it
routinely. It will not let you cook this board. It is a Class 3 registration for
low-risk food, and the things it excludes are, almost exactly, the things on the
menu.

**Is a matcha cart a business?** Yes, and on the modelling in
[19-the-cart-numbers.md](19-the-cart-numbers.md) it is the first version of this
idea that makes money. But the three channels named — farmers' markets, weddings,
corporate — are not equally open, and the first one is mostly closed for a
reason worth knowing before you plan around it.

---

## 1. The council-approved home kitchen

### It exists, and it has a name

Victorian councils register **home-based food businesses** operating out of a
domestic kitchen. Some councils issue exactly the document the phrase suggests —
Frankston, for instance, has an *Application for Home-Based Kitchen Permit*.
**[CONFIRMED]** So the thing you heard about is real, it is not a loophole, and
it is not obscure.

Registration is under the same *Food Act 1984* as any other food business. You
register with your council, they inspect, you renew annually. What differs is
the **class**, and the class is the whole story.

### What it actually permits

A domestic kitchen is accepted for **Class 3** and **Class 3A** work: low-risk
food, and pre-packaged potentially hazardous food. **[CONFIRMED]** Concretely,
the permitted list looks like:

- **Cakes and biscuits that do not contain egg, custard or cream.**
- Storage and sale of **pre-packaged** potentially hazardous food (frozen goods,
  for example).
- Warehousing and distribution of pre-packaged food.
- Serving **coffee and tea — including with dairy, soy, almond or any other
  milk — for immediate consumption.** **[CONFIRMED]**

That last line is the important one, and it is why the cart works.

### What it explicitly does not permit

Councils name these as inappropriate from a domestic kitchen **[CONFIRMED]**:

- **Catering**, including platters and sandwiches with high-risk ingredients.
- **Wholesale production to supply retail outlets.**
- Any large-scale food activity, which belongs in a commercial kitchen.
- Class 2 work generally — unpackaged, potentially hazardous, ready-to-eat food.

### Held against the board

| On the board | Home kitchen? |
| --- | --- |
| Filter, espresso, cortado, flat white, latte, cold brew | **Yes** — coffee for immediate consumption |
| All seven matcha drinks, hojicha latte | **Yes** — tea with milk, immediate consumption **[LIKELY]** |
| Iced filter, Vietnamese iced coffee, lemonade, yuzu soda | **Yes** **[LIKELY]** |
| Black sesame cookie, cardamom bun | **Probably** — if the recipe has no egg, custard or cream |
| Croissant | **Probably**, same test |
| Matcha basque cheesecake, hojicha custard tart, cake of the day | **No** — egg, custard, cream |
| Kaya toast with soft egg | **No** |
| Pho toastie | **No** |
| Singaporean chilli prawn roll | **No** |

So a council-approved home kitchen gets you **every drink on the board and
possibly two of the six desserts**. It does not get you the kitchen section, and
the kitchen section is what made the garage a Class 2 problem in the first place.

### One correction to the earlier plan

[11-alternatives.md](11-alternatives.md) suggested baking at home under a Class 3
registration and **selling wholesale to other cafes**. That is wrong, and this
research is why: councils list *wholesale production of food to supply retail
outlets* among the activities that are **not** appropriate from a domestic
kitchen. **[CONFIRMED]** Wholesale needs a commercial kitchen — your own, or
hired. The correction is carried into that file.

### So is it an option?

**As a way to run the cafe on the board: no.** It is the same conclusion as
[03-food-registration.md](03-food-registration.md), reached from the other
direction. The eggs and the prawns decide it.

**As the licensing base for a drinks business: yes, and it is the cheap
foundation the whole cart plan sits on.** No Food Safety Program, no Food Safety
Supervisor, just basic records about suppliers and potentially hazardous food.
**[CONFIRMED]**

**Ask Whitehorse directly** whether they will register a home-based Class 3
kitchen at this address, what their fee is, and whether whisked matcha is treated
as tea for classification. That last one is worth asking explicitly rather than
assuming — the guidance names tea and coffee, and matcha is powdered green tea,
but you want it in writing from the officer who will inspect you. **[VERIFY]**

## 2. The matcha cart

### How it is registered

Register a **mobile food premises** with the council where the vehicle is
**garaged** — Whitehorse — and then trade anywhere in Victoria. **[CONFIRMED]**
You are registered once, not per council.

For each public event you lodge a **Statement of Trade** through **FoodTrader**,
naming where and when. Trading at two different markets on the same weekend is
still one premises; it is two Statements of Trade. **[CONFIRMED]**

And the detail that makes the private-event side genuinely easy:

> **You do not need to lodge a Statement of Trade for an event that is not open
> to the public — such as a wedding or a work function.** **[CONFIRMED]**

So the wedding and corporate channels carry almost no per-job admin. You quote,
you turn up, you pour. That is a materially lighter operation than the market
side, and it is also where the money is.

### The three channels, honestly

**Farmers' markets — largely closed, and this is the finding to absorb.**

VFMA-accredited farmers' markets exist to sell what the stallholder grew or made.
**Resellers are not permitted at VFMA Accredited Farmers' Markets without
exception.** **[CONFIRMED]** Accreditation covers farmers within Victoria or
100 km of the border, and producers using a majority of Victorian-grown
ingredients.

A cart pouring imported Uji matcha and imported coffee is neither. You do not
grow it and you do not make it — you prepare it. Under the accreditation rules
that is a reseller.

Two caveats worth chasing rather than assuming:

- Many farmers' markets do host a coffee vendor as **market amenity** — the
  hospitality stall that keeps shoppers there — rather than as an accredited
  producer. That is a different conversation with the market manager and it is
  worth having. **[VERIFY]**
- **Community markets are a different animal entirely** and are broadly open to
  prepared-food vendors. Melbourne's east has plenty. **[CONFIRMED]**

The practical read: say **community markets**, not farmers' markets, and treat
any accredited farmers' market as a bonus you negotiate individually.

**Weddings — the strongest channel.**

No Statement of Trade. A fixed fee rather than per-head. High willingness to pay
for something guests have not seen at the last four weddings, which a properly
whisked ceremonial matcha bar genuinely is. Booked months ahead, which smooths
the calendar. Saturdays, which does not clash with a Sunday market.

**Corporate and brand activations — the best margin, the least romance.**

Office mornings, conferences, product launches, office-warmings. They pay
promptly, they book midweek, they do not haggle, and they rebook. Matcha reads as
a wellness offering to a corporate buyer, which is a category with budget.

### It is not an empty field

Real operators already do this in Melbourne. **MatchaSeekers** runs a premium
mobile matcha bar for corporate, weddings and brand activations with uniformed
staff. **Moments Event Hire** runs a combined coffee-and-matcha cart. **The Cart
Co** sells a "wellness cart". **[CONFIRMED]**

That is good news, not bad: it proves the buyers exist and it establishes the
price. But "the only matcha cart in Melbourne" is not a claim you can make, and
the pitch has to be about the matcha being **actually good** — ceremonial grade,
whisked to order, at temperature — rather than about the format being novel.

That is the same positioning the site already has:
*a quiet obsession with matcha*.

### What the market charges

Mobile coffee cart hire in Australia runs roughly **$490 to $1,200+**, with most
professional hire starting around **$750**, and some quoting from **$600 + GST**.
Pricing is per package — hours, staff, expected volume — **not per head**.
**[CONFIRMED]** One operator adds matcha to a coffee package for **+$50**.

Matcha as the *hero* rather than an add-on should sit at the upper end, because
it is the reason they booked you.

### What it costs to pour

Wholesale ceremonial matcha at about **$160/kg** is **$0.48 a serve at 3g, $0.64
at 4g, $0.80 at 5g**. **[CONFIRMED]** Minimum orders start around 1 kg, with real
wholesale rates from 5–10 kg. **[LIKELY]**

Which surfaces something about the board worth flagging separately: genuine
ceremonial matcha retails at roughly **$40–$45 for a 30g tin** in Australia, and
$60–$80 for premium single-cultivar. **[CONFIRMED]** The board sells **Matcha,
30g at $18.00**. Either that is not ceremonial grade, or it is priced at well
under half the market. Worth resolving before it goes on a cart menu —
see [12-concept-and-marketing.md](12-concept-and-marketing.md) on pricing.

The structure of it matters more than the totals: **40 market days roughly cover
the entire year of fixed costs**, which means the events are close to pure
margin. Markets keep the lights on and build the name; events are the business.

Thirty events a year is about one a fortnight. That is the number to test against
reality first, because the whole result rests on it.

### What to check before believing any of this

- **Whitehorse**: will they register a mobile food premises garaged at a
  residential address in a court, and what is the fee?
- **Whether a cart at home creates the same problems as a cafe at home** —
  customers do not come to the house, but a trailer parked in a residential court
  may have its own local-law and neighbour dimension. Ask. **[VERIFY]**
- **Insurance**: event venues and markets typically require public liability at a
  stated limit. Confirm what $10m versus $20m costs and which venues demand which.
- **Two or three community markets**: their fee, their waiting list, and whether
  they already have a coffee vendor with exclusivity. Exclusivity clauses are
  common and would stop you before anything else does. **[VERIFY]**
- **Whether whisked matcha is classified as tea** for Class 3 purposes.
