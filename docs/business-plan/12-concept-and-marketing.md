# The concept, and how anyone finds out about it

## What this actually is

*gam sia* — 感謝, thank you — is a **matcha-led, east-west cafe that opens one
morning a week**. The board already says so: seven matcha drinks against six
coffees, a kitchen of pho toasties and Singaporean chilli prawn rolls and kaya
toast, and desserts that read Nordic-Japanese rather than Melbourne-generic.

The site's own copy is the positioning, and it is good positioning:

> *The morning we wait all week for.*
> *A quiet obsession with matcha.*
> *Worth setting a Sunday alarm for.*

That is a **destination** proposition, not a convenience one. It has to be,
because nobody is walking past a cul-de-sac in Vermont South. Every single
customer arrives on purpose, having decided in advance. This single fact
determines the entire marketing plan and most of the risk.

## Why scarcity is the strategy, not the limitation

One day a week looks like a constraint imposed by a garage. Treat it instead as
the product:

- **It manufactures an occasion.** A cafe open seven days is a utility. A cafe
  open six hours a week is a plan you make with someone.
- **It concentrates demand.** The same 300 weekly customers spread over seven
  days is 43 a day and an empty room. On one Sunday it is a queue, and a queue
  is the most effective advertising a food business has ever had.
- **It protects quality.** Matcha whisked to order and croissants baked that
  morning are possible at 84 covers. They stop being possible at 500.
- **It is honest about the operator.** One person cannot run seven days. Almost
  every small cafe failure is an exhaustion story before it is a financial one.

The risk of scarcity is that it caps revenue at a level that may not cover the
fixed cost of compliance. That is exactly what [the numbers](13-the-numbers.md)
show, and it is the central tension of this whole plan.

## Who is actually going to come

Three groups, in the order they matter:

**1. The 2 km neighbourhood — Vermont South, Vermont, Forest Hill.**
Your first hundred customers and your entire first year of word of mouth. They
walk or drive three minutes. They are also, and this is not a coincidence, the
same people who can object to your planning permit. Marketing to them and
keeping them onside are the same activity. See [09-neighbours.md](09-neighbours.md).

**2. The eastern-suburbs matcha crowd.**
People who will drive 20–40 minutes for ceremonial-grade matcha whisked properly,
because very few suburban cafes do it and the ones that do are in Glen Waverley,
Box Hill or the city. This is the group that makes the concept a destination
rather than a corner shop. They are found almost entirely through Instagram and
almost entirely through images.

**3. The east-west food crowd.**
Kaya toast and pho toasties are a specific, culturally-literate offer in a part
of Melbourne with a large and food-serious Chinese, Malaysian, Singaporean and
Vietnamese population. This is your defensible menu ground; a generic cafe
cannot copy it credibly.

Deakin University's Burwood campus is close enough to matter on paper, but it is
a **Sunday** business — students are the weekday trade you will never capture.
Do not build the plan on them.

## Positioning, in one line

> The only place in the eastern suburbs where the matcha is whisked to order and
> the toastie has pho in it, open one morning a week.

Everything on the board supports that. Nothing needs adding. The most common
failure mode from here is menu creep — adding a smashed avo because someone
asked — which dissolves the only thing that makes the drive worth it.

## Channels, ranked by what they will actually deliver

**Instagram is the whole marketing plan.** For a destination food business with
a visual product and a scarcity mechanic, it is not one channel among many, it
is the channel. Post the Saturday bake and the Sunday queue. The matcha pour is
the single most shareable thing you make — build the counter so it can be
filmed. Announce closures and specials in Stories; that is what regulars will
check on Sunday morning at 07:50.

**Google Business Profile.** Free, and the first thing anyone searching "matcha
Vermont South" will see. Note the real consequence: **it publishes your home
address**, with a map pin, on the open internet, permanently. Decide that
deliberately. If you would rather not, the alternative is to stay
Instagram-and-website-only and accept slower discovery.

**The website you already have.** It does the job a cafe site should: hours,
board, address, and nothing else. Two things it should gain if this goes ahead:

- A way to say *"closed this Sunday"* without editing code — currently a
  closure means changing `config.js` and re-running the tools.
- Somewhere to state allergens, since the board carries sesame, egg, dairy,
  gluten, crustacean and tree nut and you have a legal duty to answer allergen
  questions accurately.

**Word of mouth and the neighbourhood.** For a one-day cafe, the highest-yield
marketing act in the whole plan is knocking on the twelve nearest doors *before*
you lodge anything, with a coffee and an explanation. It is simultaneously your
best customer acquisition and your best planning-permit insurance.

**What not to bother with.** Paid ads (you cannot serve the demand), delivery
platforms (20–35% commission on a menu with a 62% gross margin, and they destroy
the occasion), printed flyers, and any signage that needs a permit you do not
have — see the signs clause in [02-planning-permit.md](02-planning-permit.md).

## Pricing

The board prices are **sane to slightly cheap** for eastern-suburbs Melbourne in
2026: $3.80 for a flat white and $4.00 for a latte with oat included at no
charge is under market. Oat-free-of-charge is a genuinely good, generous
position — but it is roughly a 15–20 cent per-cup gift on well over half of the
drinks poured, and at these volumes that is real money.

Two changes worth modelling before opening:

1. **Coffee up $0.20–$0.40.** A $4.20 flat white is unremarkable in 2026 and it
   is close to pure margin. This is the single highest-leverage number on the
   board.
2. **The kitchen is underpriced for what it costs you.** The prawn roll at
   $15.00 carries the highest food cost, the longest ticket time and the most
   food-safety risk on the menu. It should either be priced at what it truly
   costs in throughput, or be a deliberate, limited, sold-out-by-eleven hero item.

Do **not** discount, do not run loyalty stamps, and do not do a "coffee club".
Scarcity and discounting cancel each other out.

## The first three months

| Phase | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Weeks −8 to −1 | Neighbours doorknocked. Instagram opened and posting the build. A soft "opening soon" on the site. Friends-and-family service run at full pace, unpaid, to find the bottlenecks. |
| Week 1 | Open quietly. Deliberately under-promote. You want 40 customers you can serve well, not 150 you cannot. |
| Weeks 2–6 | Fix the run sheet. Establish the bake quantities from the waste log. Let word of mouth do the work. |
| Weeks 6–12 | First deliberate push: the matcha story, properly photographed. Expect the first genuine queue, and have the plan for where it stands. |

Measure exactly four things: **transactions per Sunday**, **average transaction
value**, **waste as a percentage of what you baked**, and **how you felt on
Monday**. The fourth one is not a joke; it is the variable that actually decides
whether this business exists in year two.
