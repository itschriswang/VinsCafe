The plan

Whether the garage can be a cafe

This is a cafe that does not exist yet. The board is real, the hours are real, and the room is a garage on a residential court in Vermont South. What follows is the honest answer to whether that room can lawfully and profitably become the cafe, worked out properly rather than hoped at.

The verdict

Nothing about it is prohibited. It is all discretionary, expensive, and at one day a week it loses money in every version we can model.

The concept is sound. The container is wrong.

Three findings

In the order they bite

  1. A permit is required, which is worse than being banned outright

    A cafe is a food and drink premises. In the residential zone that is a use you may apply for, not one you may simply do. The application is discretionary, advertised to the neighbours, and decided on amenity, traffic, parking and neighbourhood character: the four grounds on which a cul-de-sac is weakest.

  2. This board cannot be cooked in a house kitchen

    Soft eggs, prawns, dairy and unpackaged food made to order put it in the highest ordinary risk class, and that class is not accepted in a domestic kitchen. The garage would have to become a genuine commercial one: sealed finishes, a dedicated hand basin, separate prep and wash sinks, mechanical exhaust over the grill.

  3. Six hours a week cannot carry the cost of being legal

    Insurance, registration, trade waste, grease arrestor servicing, commercial waste, compliance statements, accounting. That bill arrives whether the door opens one day a week or six, and six hours of trade is not enough to absorb it.

Growth makes it worse, not better. The moment a second pair of hands is needed, a Sunday costs more per hour than an extra pair of hands can earn back at this average sale.

Twenty

The one number worth designing around

A room that seats no more than twenty people does not have to provide customer toilets. Design for twenty and the accessible toilet, the single most expensive thing in the fitout, disappears from the drawing entirely.

It helps twice: it is also exactly the kind of self-imposed limit a council would rather be offered than have to impose.

What to do instead

Ranked

  • A cart, or a van The same business without the building. It is registered where the vehicle is kept, which is the garage, and then it trades anywhere. No change of use, no trade waste, no objections, and coffee with milk sits in a far lighter class than the kitchen does.
  • A market stall The cheapest way to find out whether strangers will queue for the matcha twice. Worth doing first whatever else is decided.
  • Somebody else's Sunday A registered cafe closed on the day we would open. The full board, unchanged, on premises that already comply.
  • Baking for other cafes The buns and the cookies, wholesale. Nobody comes to the house.
  • A shopfront The conventional answer, and it only works on more than one day a week.

The order

Cheapest answers first

The sequence matters more than the content. The temptation is to start with drawings and equipment and discover the fatal problem in month six. These come first because they are nearly free and any one of them can end it.

  1. A title searchAn old covenant forbidding trade on the land would stop the permit before the council ever saw it. About fifty dollars to find out.
  2. A call to the insurerRunning an undisclosed business from home can void the policy on the whole house, not just the part with the coffee machine in it.
  3. Four free phone callsPlanning, environmental health, the water authority, the lender. Each of them can say something that changes the answer.
  4. Then test the demandA few market days, before a single wall is lined. If the queue is not there where the foot traffic is handed to you, it will not be here.

How much to trust this. It was researched without being able to reach the source documents directly, so the clause numbers, thresholds and fees behind it come second-hand and want checking before anyone spends money. The zone this house sits in was never confirmed. It is enough to decide with. It is not enough to sign with, and it is not legal, planning or tax advice.
